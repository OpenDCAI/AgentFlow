# Phase 1: Environment Exploration Guide
* **Exploration Strategy**:
  - Begin by searching for raw seeds where the core challenge is **plans where movement and placement legality depend on adjacency, occupancy, or structures built earlier** rather than surface wording alone. First, inspect the environment description and identify which facts, resources, spatial relations, or external data fields can change during execution. For example, in a weak seed the task may look hard in prose but the decisive variable never changes, so it should be discarded immediately.
  - Force the exploration policy to uncover at least one dependency chain before you stop searching. Start from the goal, work backward to the enabling preconditions, and record which earlier actions or tool calls would be required to make those preconditions true. For example, if a later transfer needs a free gripper or a later booking needs a feasible arrival city, the trajectory must explicitly surface that enabling dependency.
  - Prefer environments that contain at least one plausible but wrong branch. During exploration, intentionally probe an attractive shortcut and note why it fails under the environment rules, because this creates a strong contrastive seed for later synthesis. For example, a route with missing transport, a placement that breaks reachability, or a reuse step attempted before cleanup makes the capability measurable rather than rhetorical.
  - Maintain an explicit scratch state while exploring instead of relying on vague memory. After every exploratory action or tool result, update a compact record of what changed, what remains unknown, and which decisions are now blocked or newly enabled. For example, after retrieving accommodation options or moving a block off a stack, you should immediately mark the newly available alternatives so the later synthesis can reconstruct a faithful trajectory.
* **Target Trajectory Profile**:
  - The target trajectory must exhibit **a trajectory where at least one early placement changes later reachability or movement options**. Concretely, the trajectory should show at least one early step that is only valuable because it enables a later step, not because it looks locally optimal in isolation. For example, cleaning a glass, freeing a gripper, checking an alternative city, or building a foothold are all good enablers if they later unlock an otherwise impossible action.
  - A good trajectory should expose both progress and risk. It should contain enough structure that one can point to the exact moment where a wrong action would have caused infeasibility, contradiction, or hallucination. For example, if changing city on the wrong day ruins the return leg or painting a certain tile seals off the remaining workspace, the seed has the right shape.
  - The trajectory must be inspectable step by step. Each action or tool call should have a legible reason tied to a visible state, retrieved fact, or constraint, so that the later QA pair can justify why that move occurred. For example, 'choose hotel B because hotel A violates minimum nights' is strong, whereas 'pick hotel B because it seems better' is too opaque.
  - The final trajectory should be neither trivial nor bloated. Aim for a sequence long enough to reveal the core capability, but short enough that every step matters and can be audited. For example, a six-to-twelve step plan with one or two meaningful branch points is usually far more useful than a twenty-step sequence padded with irrelevant actions.
