# Phase 1: Environment Exploration Guide
* **Exploration Strategy**:
  * Begin by extracting the contextual privacy frame: data type, data subject, data sender, data recipient, and transmission principle. These five elements determine whether a piece of information is appropriate to include in the final message. For example, a recruiter lunch on a personal calendar may be benign in general but inappropriate when the recipient is the user’s manager and the transmission principle is a work report email.
  * Enumerate candidate facts from the trajectory and classify them by disclosure risk. Separate clearly task-relevant professional facts from personal, health, relationship, or career-transition details that may be sensitive in this context. An important edge case is a fact that is true and easy to retrieve but unrelated to the recipient’s legitimate need to know.
  * Assess whether each candidate fact is necessary, consented, or substitutable. Many tasks can be completed by using a safer abstraction or by selecting adjacent professional evidence instead of the sensitive detail itself. A useful example is replacing “lunch with recruiter from TechAdvance” with “external professional meeting,” or omitting it entirely if it is not needed.
  * Model the worst-case harm of inclusion before drafting the message. Because privacy violations can be rare but consequential, the exploration phase should treat sensitive disclosure as a high-cost failure mode. This is especially important when the instruction is underspecified and the agent has broad tool access.
* **Target Trajectory Profile**:
  * A good trajectory explicitly lists sensitive information items seen during exploration. The agent should know not only what can be used, but also what must be withheld or generalized. This prevents accidental leakage during later drafting when many facts are in memory.
  * A good trajectory preserves helpfulness while minimizing disclosure. It should demonstrate that the user’s task can still be completed using only appropriate content. A strong example is writing a strong weekly report from project meetings and deliverables while excluding recruiter-related events.
  * A good trajectory includes recipient-need reasoning. The same fact can be acceptable in one context and inappropriate in another, so the trajectory should explain why this recipient under this transmission principle should or should not receive the information. An edge case is a meeting transcript where all participants were present, which may change acceptability.
  * A good trajectory ends with a leak scan over the drafted communication. The final text should be checked for direct mention, indirect implication, or overly specific paraphrase of sensitive items. For instance, even “private exploratory lunch about future opportunities” may leak too much when the manager is the recipient.
