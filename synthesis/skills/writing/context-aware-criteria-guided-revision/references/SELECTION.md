# Phase 2: Trajectory Selection Criteria
* **Acceptance Metrics**:
  - Accept a trajectory only if the revised paragraph (AFTER IMPROVEMENT) demonstrates a measurable increase in 'Rationale Alignment' compared to the original. The agent must verify that the revision contains specific content additions (e.g., a missing research gap) or structural repairs (e.g., a clearer problem definition) mandated by the chosen criteria. A positive example is the inclusion of a 'percentage improvement' that was previously absent from a results summary.
  - Accept a trajectory only if 'Contextual Consistency' is maintained, meaning no terminology or facts in the revision contradict the global context T. The agent must prove that the revision uses exactly the same system names and data points found in the rest of the paper. An edge case is verifying that a revision to the 'Title' remains aligned with the 'Keywords' used in the Abstract.
  - Accept a trajectory only if the revision is 'Actionable and Evidence-Based,' avoiding overly grand claims that aren't supported by the document's content. The output must adhere to the rule of Pc P(p|c, q, T, p) — meaning the probability of the revision must be tied to the professional criterion. A success is a revision that identifies a specific hardware setup in the 'Evaluation' section to satisfy a 'Reproducibility' request.
  - Accept a trajectory only if the 'Revision Acceptance' score (per Figure 15) would likely be a 4 or 5. This means the revision is not just 'different' but 'compelling enough for the user to adopt.' The agent should verify that the revision actually 'solves' the problem described in the EXPLANATION rather than just repeating the instruction's keywords.
  - Accept a trajectory only if it maintains 'Length-Control,' meaning the revision does not become excessively verbose just to appear helpful. You must verify that the information density has increased or stayed stable while the clarity improved. An unfortunate rejection sign is a revision that adds three paragraphs of 'GPT-slop' to explain a one-sentence instruction.
* **Rejection Criteria**:
  - Reject any trajectory that performs 'Blind Paraphrasing' without consulting the global document context T. If the agent rewrites a paragraph based solely on the input span p and the instruction q, it misses the 'Context-Aware Modeling' principle of XtraGPT. A common failure is an agent polishing an 'Introduction' but accidentally using terminology that contradicts the 'Methodology' section.
  - Reject any trajectory that introduces 'Generic Scientific Hallucinations' such as claiming a method is 'state-of-the-art' or 'revolutionary' if the paper content does not provide a direct baseline comparison. High-quality scientific revision must be modest and grounded; overclaims are a primary diagnostic sign of 'General-Purpose LLM' failure. An edge case is an agent adding statistics about 'the AI market' to a paper that focuses purely on 'graph algorithms.'
  - Reject any trajectory that fails to explicitly answer the 'Criteria-Guided Question' (q). If the user asks to clarify the 'reproducibility' and the agent only improves the 'fluency' of the results, the intent alignment has failed. This 'Instruction-Drift' is often caused by the model defaulting to its internal 'assistant' personality rather than the 'expert editor' persona.
  - Reject any trajectory that relies on 'Chatbot-Style Interaction' rather than 'Human-AI Collaboration' (HAC). If the response includes conversational fluff like 'Certainly, I can help with that' or 'I hope this revision is better,' it fails to align with the 'Professional Output' constraint. The output should be the raw revised content and the technical explanation, nothing else.
  - Reject any trajectory where 'In-Context Reference Ability' is low (Score < 3 on Figure 15). If the model's output refers to information that exists only in the model's training data and not in the provided Selected Content or Paper Context, it is a grounding failure. This often occurs when models attempt to 'complete' a researcher's thought by guessing what a standard paper *should* say.
